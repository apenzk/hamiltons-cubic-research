# Argument practice

*Moved out of [SKILL.md](SKILL.md) on 2026-09-02 so the cycle pays for the loop's mechanics and reaches for this when it needs the reasoning behind them. Nothing here was changed in the move; the rules are the ones step 1 used to carry inline.*

**When to open this file.** A claim is about to be written down and you want the check that catches the way it usually fails: what a claim rests on, what a structural claim read off symmetric points is really saying, how conventions are quoted, where an argument goes wrong, and what to establish before believing a bound, a piece of evidence, or a narrowing. **[SKILL.md](SKILL.md) points here from step 1** and carries none of it itself.

#### What a claim rests on

**Count a condition's strength from the symmetry of the objects, not from the shape of the formula.** "This vanishing is $k$ equations" is usually read off the expression (the dimension of an image, the number of terms) and that reading ignores the symmetries that collapse them. A quantity built from a totally antisymmetric form counts triples rather than pairs, and can vanish *identically* on a subspace too small to admit any; two conditions written differently can be the same number; a system that looks overdetermined can be empty. Before arguing that a condition is strong because it is many equations, or weak because it is few, establish the count from the symmetry, and check the degenerate low-dimensional cases, where the true count is often zero.

**Prove the property of the quantity the conclusion multiplies, not of the factor you derived.** A composed bound is a product, and its behaviour in a parameter belongs to the product, not to any factor. A factor proved to increase can sit inside a bound that decreases, because a neighbouring factor falls faster, and the two agree on whatever range was screened, which is where agreement was looked for. So before concluding from one factor's monotonicity, write the whole product and take the sign of its derivative. **The tell is a derived bound that beats the record's own standing figure**: that is a result or a dropped factor, and which comes before anything is written.

**A result inherits the status of its weakest input, and the record already labels that status.** Deriving something from a fact taken off the record is only as strong as the label that fact carries there, "presumed", "measured at these dimensions", "conditional on the conjecture". Those labels are written precisely so they can be read later, and they are the first thing skipped, because a sentence stating a fact reads the same whether or not a hedge sits beside it. The worst case is quiet: a bound derived from the very statement being pursued looks like progress and is circular. **Before using a recorded fact as a hypothesis, find its label and carry it into the conclusion**, and if the label says the fact is the open problem, the derivation has proved nothing.

#### A structural claim read off computable points is a claim about their symmetry

**The points a programme can write down are the symmetric ones**, and on a symmetric point the symmetry fixes every equivariant quantity *before* the hypothesis under test is consulted. An isotropy-irreducible point makes any equivariant symmetric $2$-tensor a multiple of the identity; a homogeneous one makes every pointwise invariant constant. So a claim holding at every computable point may be recording the catalogue's symmetry rather than any property of the space, and **this is the most expensive recurring error available, because every individual step looks sound.** What went wrong is invisible at each step and visible only in the shape: *the claim was never evaluated anywhere the symmetry was absent.*

**So name the feature the sample happens to share, and evaluate at a point that lacks it.** One anisotropic example is worth more than a large random sweep and costs less, because random sampling of a structured class varies everything except the thing that matters. Where no such point can be constructed, the honest record is that the claim holds *on the symmetric class*, with that scope in the statement rather than a footnote, and the next question is whether that class is the one the theorem needs. **When the target turns out to be a theorem on every constructible point, all remaining difficulty lies outside them and further work on the catalogue is measurement of a settled region.**

#### An invariant of a chosen basis decides nothing about the operator

**Before an invariant computed from a chosen eigenbasis is used to decide a property of an operator, check that the operator determines that basis.** A degenerate eigenvalue makes every orthonormal basis of its eigenspace an eigenbasis, so the quantity is a fact about the presentation, and it varies freely while the operator stays fixed. **The witness this misses satisfies every equation the test imposes, carries the diagnostic's own value, and has the property anyway in a basis nobody wrote down.** *So establish uniqueness of the basis first, or state the conclusion about the presented data and say so in the claim.*

#### Definitions and conventions are quoted, never restated

**A quantity's name is not its definition; check the convention before quoting a number.** A long-lived project accumulates several functions computing "the same" quantity in different normalisations, a factor from bracketing in one representation rather than another, a square root, a sign. They agree on the family where they were first written and diverge elsewhere, so picking one by name gives a number that is right in the wrong units, and the error is invisible because the value still looks plausible. **Before a figure enters the record, confirm which definition the claim needs and which the code computes**, and if the repository holds more than one, say which was used. Where two implementations disagree, the disagreement is itself a result: it locates the boundary of the family where the definitions coincide.

**When a report indicts existing code, run both and compare, then keep one.** A reimplementation written from the report's description inherits whatever that description misread, and the two then agree for the wrong reason: agreement between a report and a fresh implementation of the report is the same reading twice. So run the indicted code and the new one on the same inputs and compare. Disagreement localises the fault at once. *This is the one place a reimplementation earns its cost.*

**Restating a definition is where errors enter; quote the code instead.** A brief, a note or a write-up that paraphrases the project's own conventions (an inner product, a normalisation, an index convention) introduces a version that nothing checks, and it will be reproduced in the next brief and the one after. The implementation is the definition of record. Point at it, or copy the line verbatim, and reserve prose for what the convention is *for*.

Then work the task to completion. Keep going across sub-steps rather than stopping at the first partial result, a cycle ends when the task is settled or provably blocked, and "provably blocked" means a named obstruction, not fatigue.

**A stored source without a transcription is a source nobody has read.** A reference filed with only its PDF and a raw text dump reads as consulted while its theorems stay invisible, and the identification that reframes a problem can sit unopened in it for the whole life of the project. When a cycle stalls, check whether the sources already in [references/](../../../references/) carry notes before searching for new ones.

#### Prefer the bound to the computation, for its failure mode

**Given a choice between deriving a quantity in coordinates and bounding it by a structural property, take the bound.** Not for elegance: **a bound that is invariant under a convention cannot be broken by getting that convention wrong**, while a coordinate computation carries every convention it touched.

**A chain that computes a coefficient block by block falls to a sign convention or an omitted perturbation term; a bound on the same coefficient by a range-length or an annihilation property survives both.**

**The rule bites only where a genuine choice exists**, and it makes work robust rather than making errors visible, so it ranks below the checks that expose a defect, and above nothing.

#### Where an argument goes wrong

**When several attempts fail the same way, change the kind of argument rather than the argument.** A run of failures sharing a mechanism (all from symmetry, all from bounding, all from sampling) is evidence about the class, not about the instances. Ask what kind of structure has not been tried at all.

**A proven negative is recorded as a measurement until it has been audited; only then is it written as a route-closing claim.** Ordinary findings are recorded and moved past, and a wrong one costs the cycle that finds it. A negative that closes a route costs every cycle after it, because nobody reopens a closed route to check, which makes recording one the highest-variance action in the loop. The failure mode is specific and it feels like diligence: a computation returns a negative, the reason is written up in the same breath, and it goes into the record as settled while the reasoning behind it is the least tested thing in the cycle. **So state what was measured, mark it as a candidate, and let the audit decide whether it closes anything.** Two symptoms say the candidate is weak and both are cheap to check: the negative rests on a *proxy* for the quantity of interest rather than the quantity itself, and its stated consequence would be unchanged if the computation had come out the other way.

**A result that is true but changes nothing is a finding, and it is recorded as one.** Proving a statement that turns out inert downstream closes off a route, which is what stops a later cycle spending itself on the same idea. State plainly what it establishes and where it fails to reach, and put it through Gate 2 like any other result, an inert proof accepted as a useful one is worse than no proof.

#### Before believing a bound or a relaxation

**When a maximisation's optimum is known to be forced, exploit that before bounding anything.** A configuration pinned by an earlier result collapses several free constants into one object, and quantities that had to be bounded separately turn out to be evaluated at the same point, where they frequently cannot both be extremal. Ask what the optimiser is already known to do before assuming the worst case at every slot independently.

**Enumerate what the true object satisfies before judging a relaxation too weak.** A relaxation is only as strong as the constraint list it carries, so a witness that beats the target proves the *list* is incomplete, not that the route is dead. Write down every property the real object has (the identities its defining equation forces, the traces that vanish, the normalisations) and check the witness against each before recording a negative. Closing a live route is the most expensive error available, because nobody reopens it.

#### Before believing evidence

**When a result localises a quantity to a subspace, verify the subspace before believing the localisation.** A construction that over- or under-shoots the intended span still returns a number, and the number looks like a discovery. Check the subspace against an independent invariant, its dimension, its decomposition into blocks, its behaviour at a point where the answer is known, before recording that the phenomenon lives there.

**A run in a regime where the answer is already known is a control, not a test, say which one it is before reading the output.** The cheap dimensions, the small cases and the well-studied corner are the ones an implementation can reach first, and a result there that matches the record confirms the *instrument* rather than the hypothesis. That is worth having and worth reporting as such; what it must not do is stand in for the expensive run in the regime the claim is actually about. Name, before the run, which regime would decide the question, and record any result outside it as a control with the reason it cannot decide.

**Check which direction a bound has to go before computing it.** An ascent finds a lower bound on a supremum; a certificate needs the upper one. Solving an inner maximisation exactly and ascending the outer one still returns a lower bound, which certifies nothing however exact the inner step was. Before running an optimisation, write down whether the number it returns bounds the target from above or below, and whether that is the side the argument needs.

#### Before believing a narrowing

**"The constraints leave only this" is a claim to falsify before it is written down.** A derivation that narrows a set to a single candidate is asserting that nothing else satisfies the stated conditions, and that assertion is checked by *trying to violate it*: construct a second object meeting every condition written down, and see whether it also meets the conclusion. When it does not, the conditions on the page are weaker than the ones being used in the author's head, the commonest form being a bound quoted at its extreme case while the argument silently assumes the extreme is forced. **The construction is usually three lines and it is the only step that can catch this**, since re-reading the derivation reproduces the same gap. Do it before the claim reaches the record, not after: a false narrowing inside a proof reads exactly like a true one.

**The task inherited from the last cycle is a claim, and it is falsified before it is worked on.** The rule above applies to narrowings written *during* a cycle; the expensive version is a narrowing written in a previous one, because by then it reads as the assignment rather than as an assertion. A record that says a problem "reduces to" or "is equivalently" something is asserting that the two have the same truth value, and the second form is almost always narrower, it was reached by adding a hypothesis that felt harmless. **Before spending a cycle on the reduced form, construct an object satisfying the original statement and check whether it satisfies the reduced one.** Where it does not, the cycle's real target is the gap, and everyone who searched the reduced form was searching a proper subset. A whole review round can be spent auditing a question that was the wrong question, since a brief inherits the narrowing intact.

**A bound that is tight at the extremum is the original problem, not a relaxation of it.** Replacing a hard maximisation by a pointwise upper bound looks like progress the moment the bound is easier to write down, and the question of how much was given away goes unasked. Evaluate the bound at the known or conjectured extremiser before adopting it: if it is tight there, its global supremum equals the original one and proving it is at least as hard, usually strictly harder, since it must also hold at every non-extremal point where the bound is slack. **The cheap test is one evaluation**, and it decides whether the reformulation is a route or a restatement. A reformulation can still earn its place by connecting the problem to machinery that already exists, but that is a different claim and it is stated separately.

**A structural property of a new construction is checked on the construction, not inferred from its parts.** Building an object out of understood pieces invites the assumption that the pieces' properties survive the assembly, additivity, a sign, a bound, a symmetry. Whether they do is a fact about *how the pieces were joined*, and the joining is usually the novel part: a functional that is additive over disjoint pieces is not additive over overlapping ones, and overlap is frequently the very feature that made the construction interesting. So the property most likely to be assumed is the one the construction was designed to break. **Evaluate it once on the finished object**, the object already exists, the check is one call, and the alternative is an assertion that reaches the record beside carefully proved statements and is indistinguishable from them.

**A proof closes one direction; name it, and check that the claim it was pursued for needs that one.** The direction rule above is usually read as being about computing, and the same error survives it in a later form: a result genuinely proved, then described as settling the question it was pursued for, when it settles the half running the opposite way. Exhibiting an object bounds a maximum from below and a multiplicity from below; showing nothing *else* has a property is an upper bound, and no exhibited object supplies one. When recording a proof, write the sentence that says which of the two it is, and re-read the open problem to see which one that problem needs, the gap is easiest to miss in the cycle that produced the proof, when the result is most convincing.

**A step named as the weak point does not get used as though it were sound.** Writing the doubt down (in a reviewer's brief, a caveat, a note to return to) feels like discharging it, and the argument then proceeds across the same step in the same breath. The flag has to change what gets asserted, not only what gets audited: when a step is identified as the one most likely to fail, the claim resting on it is stated as conditional on that step, or the step is settled first. An inference used in the direction that was just called unsound is the most likely thing in the cycle to be wrong, because it has already been examined and still could not be justified.

**A theorem's unnamed premises fail more often than its stated hypotheses.** The hypotheses a structural theorem is usually quoted with are the ones that get checked, because they are the ones written down; the premise that the object is of the right kind at all, that the map goes from the space to itself, that the form is defined on the whole domain, that the set is closed under the operation, is carried silently and is where the argument actually breaks. Before invoking the theorem, write down what kind of object it needs and verify that first, separately from the properties it is being invoked for. An argument whose named conditions all check out and whose conclusion is false has almost always lost a premise nobody stated.

#### Argument hygiene

**A hole you can name is a hole you can close, and writing it down anywhere is not closing it.** Handing a known weakness to the adversarial review feels like diligence, and it spends the reviewers' budget confirming something a finished self-check would have settled, budget that was the only chance of finding what nobody had thought of. Where the gap is a partial enumeration, a tolerance, a case not run, close it before the brief goes out; put in the brief the things that cannot be checked from inside the work. **The same illusion works through the record**: entering a gap in a docstring, a plan item or a limitations note makes it feel handled, because the record is now consistent, and a consistent record is what a finished unit feels like. Consistency of the record is not progress on the problem.

**Expand to an identity first, then let the hypotheses enter at named points.** A computation that carries every hypothesis from the first line mixes what is true of all objects with what is true of these, and when it fails there is no way to see which assumption was doing the work. Write the general expansion with no hypotheses at all, specialise one argument, and only then apply each condition, the good outcome is being able to say that one hypothesis was used exactly once, at one step, and another twice in the single surviving case. That form is short enough to check by hand and it locates immediately what a counterexample would have to break.

**Computing in a basis of a subspace silently applies the projection onto it.** Expressing an operator in coordinates adapted to the subspace under study is the natural thing to do and it changes the object: what gets measured is the projected operator, which can have properties (being scalar, being symmetric, preserving a decomposition) that the original lacks entirely. The measurement then confirms a claim about something other than the thing claimed. When a result depends on an operator's behaviour rather than on a subspace's, compute it in the ambient basis at least once and check the image really lands where it is assumed to.

**A construction that returns zero has measured something; read it before trying another one.** When the natural candidate for a test vector, a generator or a deformation comes out identically zero, the instinct is to treat it as a failed guess and reach for a different one. But an exact zero is a statement (the object lies in the stabiliser, the kernel, the annihilator of what was applied) and that statement usually names the construction that will work. Ask what has to be true for the vanishing to happen, and build from the answer rather than from the next guess.

**A diagnosis is a claim, and it needs its own evidence.** Finding that something is broken, and finding why, are two results; the first is usually solid and the second is usually a guess made while relieved. The guess then travels (into the record, into the next brief, into the rule written to prevent a recurrence) and it is rarely revisited, because the symptom really was there and the fix really did work. Neither of those confirms the cause. State the mechanism only if it was tested as a claim in its own right, and otherwise say what was observed and that the reason is not established.

**A constant taken from a convention is taken from the definition instead.** Normalisations with names (an index, a dual Coxeter number, a trace form) come in several conventions that differ by small integer factors, and a rule quoting one reaches the right answer only for whoever remembers which. The definition has no convention: compute the constant as the ratio the object actually determines, and the factor cannot enter. The signal that this is needed is the same rule appearing twice in a record with different constants, both producing correct numbers.

**A constant that caps something is set at or above what it caps.** Rounding a bounding constant down turns an upper bound into a statement that is not one, and the verdicts often survive anyway, which is why the error persists unnoticed.
