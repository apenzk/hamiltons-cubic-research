## Conventions used

$\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is the **compact** real form: real $n\times n$ matrices with $X^{\mathsf T}=-X$, with the bracket $[X,Y]=XY-YX$ and the negative-definite-Killing convention of [conventions.md](../findings/conventions.md) ($B(X,Y)=(n-2)\operatorname{tr}(XY)$). $V=\mathbb R^n$ is the vector representation. For $g\in O(n)$ write $\mathrm{Ad}_g(X)=gXg^{-1}=gXg^{\mathsf T}$. An **involution** is $\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(n))$ with $\sigma^2=\mathrm{id}$ (the identity is admitted, as the trivial grading). $\mathrm{Inn}(\mathfrak g)$ is the group generated by $e^{\mathrm{ad}X}$.

The only normalisation-sensitive quantity below is a dimension count, and dimensions are convention-free.

## Step 0, gradings and involutions are the same data

A $\mathbb Z_2$-grading is a vector-space splitting $\mathfrak g=\mathfrak g_0\oplus\mathfrak g_1$ with $[\mathfrak g_i,\mathfrak g_j]\subseteq\mathfrak g_{i+j\bmod 2}$. Given one, $\sigma:=+1$ on $\mathfrak g_0$ and $-1$ on $\mathfrak g_1$ is linear, bijective, satisfies $\sigma[X,Y]=[\sigma X,\sigma Y]$ on each of the four bidegree cases, and squares to the identity. Given an involution $\sigma$, its $\pm1$ eigenspaces (the field is $\mathbb R$ and $\sigma^2=1$, so $\mathfrak g$ splits) grade $\mathfrak g$. The two constructions are mutually inverse. So **enumerating $\mathbb Z_2$-gradings up to conjugacy is exactly enumerating involutions up to conjugacy**, and $\mathfrak g_0=\mathfrak g^\sigma$.

*Hypotheses used: none beyond $\mathrm{char}\ne2$.*

## Step 1, the vector representation is absolutely irreducible for $n\ge3$

The complexification $\mathbb C^n$ is an irreducible $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$-module for every $n\ge3$. For $n\ne4$ this is the standard fundamental representation of $B_m$ or $D_m$ with highest weight $\varpi_1$; for $n=4$, $\mathfrak{so}(4,\mathbb C)=\mathfrak{sl}_2\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and $\mathbb C^4$ is the outer tensor product $(2)\boxtimes(2)$, irreducible. It fails at $n=2$, where $\mathfrak{so}(2)$ is abelian and $\mathbb C^2$ splits into the two characters $\pm i$.

Two consequences, both used repeatedly below.

- **(1a) The commutant of $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ in $\mathrm{End}(\mathbb R^n)$ is $\mathbb R\cdot I$.** A real endomorphism commuting with the whole algebra complexifies to one commuting with $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ on $\mathbb C^n$, hence is a scalar by Schur, hence a real scalar.
- **(1b) The invariant symmetric bilinear form on $\mathbb R^n$ is unique up to a real scalar**, by (1a) applied to the endomorphism relating two such forms.

*Hypotheses used: $n\ge3$. This is the first place $n\ge3$ is load-bearing, and it is where $n=2$ dies.*

## Step 2, an automorphism preserving the vector representation is $\mathrm{Ad}_g$ with $g$ orthogonal

Let $\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(n))$ and suppose the pulled-back representation $\rho\circ\varphi$ on $\mathbb R^n$ is isomorphic to $\rho$ itself. Then there is $h\in GL(n,\mathbb R)$ with $\varphi(X)=hXh^{-1}$ for every $X$.

Now $\varphi(X)$ is skew for every skew $X$: transposing $hXh^{-1}$ gives $h^{-\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}h^{\mathsf T}=-h^{-\mathsf T}Xh^{\mathsf T}$, and setting this equal to $-hXh^{-1}$ yields $(h^{\mathsf T}h)X(h^{\mathsf T}h)^{-1}=X$ for every $X\in\mathfrak{so}(n)$. By (1a), $h^{\mathsf T}h=\lambda I$; it is positive definite, so $\lambda>0$; replacing $h$ by $h/\sqrt\lambda$ leaves $\mathrm{Ad}_h$ unchanged and makes $h$ orthogonal.

Conversely $\mathrm{Ad}_g$ for $g\in O(n)$ is an automorphism, and $\mathrm{Ad}_g=\mathrm{Ad}_{g'}$ iff $g^{-1}g'$ centralises $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, i.e. by (1a) iff $g'=\pm g$. So $\mathrm{Ad}:O(n)\to\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(n))$ has kernel exactly $\{\pm I\}$ and $PO(n):=O(n)/\{\pm I\}$ embeds.

*Hypotheses used: $n\ge3$ (through (1a)); positive definiteness of the standard form on $\mathbb R^n$, which is where **compactness** first enters, for $\mathfrak{so}(p,q)$ the same computation gives $h^{\mathsf T}Jh=\lambda J$ with $J$ indefinite and $\lambda$ of either sign.*

## Step 3, every automorphism preserves the vector representation, except at $n=8$

This is the deep step. It is proved by bounding $\mathrm{Out}(\mathfrak g):=\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak g)/\mathrm{Inn}(\mathfrak g)$ from above by the Dynkin diagram automorphism group. **Only the upper bound is needed**, the surjectivity half of the usual statement (every diagram automorphism is realised) is not used anywhere in the positive direction, which keeps the isomorphism theorem out of the argument.

### 3.1 $\mathrm{Der}(\mathfrak g)=\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak g$ and $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak g)^\circ=\mathrm{Inn}(\mathfrak g)$

Let $\mathfrak g$ be semisimple. $\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak g$ is an ideal of $\mathfrak D:=\mathrm{Der}\,\mathfrak g$, because $[D,\mathrm{ad}X]=\mathrm{ad}(DX)$. The Killing form $\kappa_{\mathfrak D}$ restricted to the ideal $\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak g$ equals the Killing form of $\mathfrak g$, which is nondegenerate; let $\mathfrak c$ be its $\kappa_{\mathfrak D}$-orthogonal complement. Then $\mathfrak c$ is an ideal and $\mathfrak c\cap\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak g=0$, so $[\mathfrak c,\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak g]=0$; for $D\in\mathfrak c$ this says $\mathrm{ad}(DX)=0$ for all $X$, i.e. $DX\in Z(\mathfrak g)=0$, i.e. $D=0$. Hence $\mathfrak D=\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak g$. Since $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak g)$ is a closed subgroup of $GL(\mathfrak g)$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak D$, its identity component is generated by $\exp(\mathrm{ad}\,\mathfrak g)$, which is $\mathrm{Inn}(\mathfrak g)$.

$\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is semisimple for $n\ge3$ and simple for $n\ge3$, $n\ne4$.

### 3.2 $\mathrm{Out}(\mathfrak g)$ injects into the diagram automorphisms

Let $\mathfrak g$ be compact simple, $\mathfrak t$ a maximal abelian subalgebra, $\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak g)$.

1. $\varphi(\mathfrak t)$ is again maximal abelian, and maximal abelian subalgebras of a compact $\mathfrak g$ are $\mathrm{Inn}$-conjugate (the standard maximal-torus conjugacy theorem, **this is where compactness is used a second time**). Composing $\varphi$ with an inner automorphism, assume $\varphi(\mathfrak t)=\mathfrak t$.
2. $\varphi$ then permutes the roots of $\mathfrak g^{\mathbb C}$ relative to $\mathfrak t^{\mathbb C}$ and preserves the Killing form, so it acts on $\mathfrak t^*$ as an automorphism of the root system. The Weyl group is realised inside $\mathrm{Inn}$ by $\mathrm{Ad}(N(T))$, so after a further inner composition $\varphi$ preserves a chosen positive system, hence permutes the simple roots preserving Cartan integers: it induces a **Dynkin diagram automorphism** $\pi$.
3. If $\pi=\mathrm{id}$, then $\varphi$ fixes every root, hence acts trivially on $\mathfrak t^*$ (roots span $\mathfrak t^*$ for semisimple $\mathfrak g$), hence fixes $\mathfrak t^{\mathbb C}$ pointwise. So $\varphi(e_\alpha)=c_\alpha e_\alpha$ for root vectors. From $[e_\alpha,e_\beta]=N_{\alpha\beta}e_{\alpha+\beta}$ with $N_{\alpha\beta}\ne0$ whenever $\alpha+\beta$ is a root, and from $[e_\alpha,e_{-\alpha}]=h_\alpha$ being fixed, one gets $c_\alpha c_\beta=c_{\alpha+\beta}$ and $c_\alpha c_{-\alpha}=1$: the assignment $\alpha\mapsto c_\alpha$ is a homomorphism from the root lattice $Q$ to $\mathbb C^\times$. Because $\varphi$ preserves the compact real form it commutes with the conjugation $\tau$ sending $e_\alpha\mapsto-e_{-\alpha}$, giving $\overline{c_\alpha}=c_{-\alpha}=c_\alpha^{-1}$, so $|c_\alpha|=1$. Thus $c\in\mathrm{Hom}(Q,U(1))$, which is precisely the adjoint torus $T_{\mathrm{ad}}$, and $\varphi=\mathrm{Ad}(t)\in\mathrm{Inn}(\mathfrak g)$.

So $\mathrm{Out}(\mathfrak g)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(\text{Dynkin diagram})$.

*Hypotheses used: $\mathfrak g$ compact (steps 1 and 3) and simple, i.e. $n\ge3$, $n\ne4$. The case $n=4$ is done separately in §3.4.*

### 3.3 Reading the diagrams

$\varphi$ carries an irreducible $\mathfrak g^{\mathbb C}$-module of highest weight $\lambda$ to one of highest weight $\pi^{-1}\lambda$, where $\pi$ acts on fundamental weights through the node permutation. So $\rho\circ\varphi\cong\rho$ over $\mathbb C$ exactly when $\pi$ fixes the node carrying the vector representation; and over $\mathbb C$ implies over $\mathbb R$, since $\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathfrak g}(V,W)\otimes_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathfrak g^{\mathbb C}}(V_{\mathbb C},W_{\mathbb C})$ and both modules are absolutely irreducible by Step 1.

| $n$ | type | $\mathrm{Aut}(\text{diagram})$ | vector node | preserved? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $3$ | $A_1$ | trivial | the only node | **yes** |
| $2m+1\ge5$ | $B_m$, $m\ge2$ | trivial, the arrow on the double bond, and for $B_2$ the asymmetry of the Cartan integers $-1$ and $-2$, forbid any swap | node 1 | **yes** |
| $6$ | $D_3=A_3$ | $\mathbb Z_2$, reversing the path | the middle node (under $\mathfrak{so}(6)\cong\mathfrak{su}(4)$ the vector representation is $\Lambda^2\mathbb C^4$) | **yes** |
| $8$ | $D_4$ | $S_3$, permuting the three outer nodes | one of the three outer nodes | **NO** |
| $2m\ge10$ | $D_m$, $m\ge5$ | $\mathbb Z_2$, swapping nodes $m-1$ and $m$ | node 1, and $1<m-1$ | **yes** |

### 3.4 The case $n=4$, where $\mathfrak{so}(4)$ is not simple

$\mathfrak{so}(4)=\mathfrak{su}(2)_+\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)_-$, the summands being the self-dual and anti-self-dual bivectors. The two summands are the only simple ideals, so every automorphism permutes them, and $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(4))=(\mathrm{Aut}\,\mathfrak{su}(2)\times\mathrm{Aut}\,\mathfrak{su}(2))\rtimes\mathbb Z_2$. Since $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{su}(2))=\mathrm{Inn}(\mathfrak{su}(2))=SO(3)$ (the $A_1$ diagram has no automorphism, and §3.2 applies to $\mathfrak{su}(2)$), $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(4))$ has exactly two components with identity component $SO(3)\times SO(3)=\mathrm{Ad}(SO(4))$. The Hodge star anticommutes with orientation reversal, so any $g\in O(4)$ with $\det g=-1$ swaps the two summands and realises the nonidentity component. Hence $\mathrm{Ad}(O(4))=\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(4))$, i.e. **the conclusion of Step 3 holds at $n=4$ as well**, by a direct argument rather than by the diagram.

### 3.5 Conclusion of Step 3

Combining Steps 2 and 3:

> **Proposition 1.** For every $n\ge3$ with $n\ne8$, the map $\mathrm{Ad}:O(n)\to\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(n))$ is surjective with kernel $\{\pm I\}$, so $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(n))\cong PO(n)$.

For odd $n$ one has $O(n)=SO(n)\times\{\pm I\}$, so $PO(n)\cong SO(n)$ and every automorphism is inner. For even $n\ne8$, $PO(n)$ has two components and the outer one is realised by any reflection.

## Step 4, $\sigma^2=\mathrm{id}$ forces $g^2=\pm I$

Let $n\ge3$, $n\ne8$, and let $\sigma$ be an involution. Write $\sigma=\mathrm{Ad}_g$, $g\in O(n)$. Then $\mathrm{Ad}_{g^2}=\mathrm{id}$, so $g^2$ centralises $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, so $g^2=\lambda I$ by (1a), and $g^2\in O(n)$ forces $\lambda^2=1$, i.e. $g^2=\pm I$. The two possibilities are exclusive unless $g=\pm I$, i.e. $\sigma=\mathrm{id}$.

*Hypotheses used: $n\ge3$.*

## Step 5, the branch $g^2=+I$

Then $g=g^{-1}=g^{\mathsf T}$ is symmetric orthogonal, so $\mathbb R^n=V_-\oplus V_+$ orthogonally with $g=-1$ on $V_-$ ($\dim p$) and $+1$ on $V_+$ ($\dim q$), $p+q=n$. A skew $X$ commutes with $g$ iff it preserves both eigenspaces, so

$$\mathfrak{so}(n)^\sigma=\mathfrak{so}(V_-)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(V_+)\cong\mathfrak{so}(p)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(q),\qquad \mathfrak g_1\cong V_-\otimes V_+,\ \dim\mathfrak g_1=pq.$$

Dimension check: $\binom p2+\binom q2+pq=\binom{p+q}2$. Two such $g$ with the same $p$ are $O(n)$-conjugate (equal eigenvalue multiplicities), so the class depends only on $p$; and $\mathrm{Ad}_g=\mathrm{Ad}_{-g}$ exchanges $(p,q)$ with $(q,p)$, so normalise $p\le q$, i.e. $0\le p\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$, with $p=0$ the identity.

## Step 6, the branch $g^2=-I$

Then $g^{\mathsf T}=g^{-1}=-g$: $g$ is a **skew** orthogonal matrix, i.e. an orthogonal complex structure.

- **$n$ must be even.** $\det(g)^2=\det(g^2)=\det(-I)=(-1)^n$, and $\det g$ is real, so $(-1)^n=+1$. *This is the point at which the **real field** produces the second family and confines it to even $n$: over $\mathbb C$ the eigenvalues $\pm i$ of $g$ are available at every $n$, but the real determinant argument still forces $n$ even, and the fixed algebra there is $\mathfrak{gl}(m,\mathbb C)$ rather than its compact form $\mathfrak u(m)$.*
- **All such $g$ are $O(n)$-conjugate.** Pick a unit $v_1$; $\langle v_1,gv_1\rangle=0$ since $g$ is skew, so $\{v_1,gv_1\}$ is orthonormal and spans a $g$-invariant plane; its orthogonal complement is $g$-invariant ($g$ orthogonal); induct. This builds an orthonormal basis $e_1,f_1,\dots,e_m,f_m$ with $ge_i=f_i$, $gf_i=-e_i$, i.e. conjugates $g$ to the standard $J_0$.
- **The fixed algebra is $\mathfrak u(m)$, $m=n/2$.** Making $\mathbb R^{2m}$ into $\mathbb C^m$ with $i$ acting as $g$, a real endomorphism commuting with $g$ is exactly a $\mathbb C$-linear one; requiring it also to be real-skew and to commute with $g$ makes it skew-Hermitian for the Hermitian form $h(u,v)=\langle u,v\rangle+i\langle u,gv\rangle$. So $\mathfrak{so}(2m)^\sigma=\mathfrak{so}(2m)\cap\mathfrak{gl}(m,\mathbb C)=\mathfrak u(m)$, of dimension $m^2$. Then $\dim\mathfrak g_1=m(2m-1)-m^2=m(m-1)=2\binom m2=\dim_{\mathbb R}\Lambda^2_{\mathbb C}\mathbb C^m$, and indeed $\mathfrak g_1\cong\Lambda^{2,0}\oplus\Lambda^{0,2}$ as a real $\mathfrak u(m)$-module.

## Step 7, the two families are distinct, and the fixed algebra separates the classes

$\mathrm{Ad}_{J_0}$ is never $O(n)$-conjugate to any $\mathrm{Ad}_{g_p}$: in $PO(n)$ the classes are distinguished by the complex spectra $\{\pm i\ (\times m)\}$ versus $\{(-1)^{\times p},1^{\times q}\}$ and $\{(-1)^{\times q},1^{\times p}\}$, which never agree.

Within the first family, put $f(p):=\binom p2+\binom{n-p}2=\dim\mathfrak g_0$. Then $f(p)-f(p+1)=n-2p-1>0$ for $0\le p<\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ and $=1$ at $p=n/2-1$ when $n$ is even, so $f$ is **strictly decreasing** on $0\le p\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$: distinct $p$ give distinct dimensions, hence distinct classes.

Across the families, when is $\mathfrak u(m)\cong\mathfrak{so}(p)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(q)$, $p+q=2m$, $p\le m$? The centre of $\mathfrak u(m)$ is one-dimensional and $\mathfrak{su}(m)$ is simple ($m\ge2$). The centre of $\mathfrak{so}(k)$ is $0$ for $k\in\{0,1\}$ and $k\ge3$, and one-dimensional for $k=2$; so exactly one of $p,q$ equals $2$. With $p\le m$ that forces $p=2$, $q=2m-2$, $m\ge3$ (and $m=3$ fails at once, $\mathfrak{so}(4)$ having two simple summands against $\mathfrak{su}(3)$'s one). For $m\ge4$ we need $\mathfrak{su}(m)\cong\mathfrak{so}(2m-2)$, so $m^2-1=(m-1)(2m-3)$, i.e. $m^2-5m+4=0$, i.e. $m\in\{1,4\}$. So:

> **Proposition 2.** For $n\ne8$, the isomorphism class of $\mathfrak g_0$ determines the $O(n)$-conjugacy class of the involution. At $n=8$ it does not: $\mathfrak u(4)\cong\mathfrak{so}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(6)$ (both $\mathbb R\oplus\mathfrak{su}(4)$), while the two involutions are not $O(8)$-conjugate.

**Dimension alone is a weaker invariant and it fails infinitely often.** Writing $p=m-k$, $q=m+k$ gives the exact identity

$$f(m-k)=\tbinom{m-k}2+\tbinom{m+k}2=m^2-m+k^2,$$

so $f(m-k)=\dim\mathfrak u(m)=m^2$ precisely when $k^2=m$. Hence $\dim\mathfrak g_0$ collides between the two families **exactly when $m=n/2$ is a perfect square**: $m=4$ ($n=8$, $p=2$, dimension $16$) and $m=9$ ($n=18$, $p=6$: $\dim(\mathfrak{so}(6)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(12))=15+66=81=\dim\mathfrak u(9)$), then $m=16$ ($n=32$, $p=12$), and so on. At $n=18$ and beyond the algebras are still non-isomorphic by Proposition 2, but **a consumer that separates gradings by $\dim\mathfrak g_0$ alone is wrong at $n=18$**, and that is a live trap at the dimensions this record works in.

## Step 8, the exceptions, one by one

### $n=2$: excluded, and rightly

$\mathfrak{so}(2)=\mathbb R$ is abelian, $\mathrm{Aut}=\mathbb R^\times$, and $\mathrm{Ad}:O(2)\to\mathrm{Aut}$ has image $\{\pm1\}$ and kernel $SO(2)$, so Proposition 1 fails badly. The involution statement nevertheless survives in a degenerate form: the only involutions are $\pm1$, with fixed algebras $\mathbb R=\mathfrak u(1)$ and $0=\mathfrak{so}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(1)$. Step 1 is what dies here.

### $n=3$: survives, list has two entries

$\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is simple, $\mathrm{Aut}=\mathrm{Inn}=SO(3)=PO(3)$. $n$ is odd so Step 6 is empty. The classes are $p=0$ (identity, $\mathfrak g_0=\mathfrak{so}(3)$) and $p=1$ ($\mathfrak g_0=\mathfrak{so}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(2)=\mathbb R$, $\dim\mathfrak g_1=2$). Under $\mathfrak{so}(3)\cong\mathfrak{su}(2)$ this is the single symmetric pair $(\mathfrak{su}(2),\mathfrak u(1))$, i.e. $S^2$. **Statement survives verbatim.**

### $n=4$: survives, despite $\mathfrak{so}(4)$ not being simple

§3.4 gives $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(4))=PO(4)$, so Steps 4–6 run unchanged. The classes are $p=1$ ($\mathfrak{so}(3)$, $\dim3$), $p=2$ ($\mathfrak{so}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(2)$, $\dim2$), and $\mathfrak u(2)$ ($\dim4$), plus the identity. **Statement survives verbatim**, and the failure of simplicity costs only the proof route, not the conclusion. It is however the smallest place where $SO(n)$- and $O(n)$-conjugacy part company; see §9.

### $n=8$: the statement is FALSE as written

**The falsifying object.** Let $\Delta$ be the real $8$-dimensional spin representation of $\mathfrak{so}(7)$. It exists: the even Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}^0_{0,7}\cong\mathrm{Cl}_{0,6}\cong M_8(\mathbb R)$ (dimension check $2^6=64=8^2$), whose unique simple module is $\mathbb R^8$; $\mathfrak{so}(7)$ sits inside $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ as the span of the $e_ie_j$, and by compactness $\Delta$ carries an invariant inner product, so we get an embedding $\mathfrak k:=\mathfrak{spin}(7)\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{so}(8)=\mathfrak{so}(\Delta)$, with $\Delta$ irreducible over $\mathbb R$.

Let $\mathfrak m$ be the orthogonal complement of $\mathfrak k$ in $\mathfrak{so}(8)$ for the (negative-definite) Killing form; $\dim\mathfrak m=28-21=7$ and $[\mathfrak k,\mathfrak m]\subseteq\mathfrak m$ by invariance. As a $\mathfrak k$-module, $\mathfrak m$ has no trivial summand: a trivial summand would be a nonzero $2$-form centralised by $\mathfrak k$, placing $\mathfrak k$ inside the stabiliser of a nonzero $2$-form, whose dimension is at most $\dim\mathfrak u(4)=16<21$. The smallest nontrivial irreducible of $\mathfrak{so}(7)$ has dimension $7$, so $\mathfrak m\cong V_7$, the vector representation. Now the bracket $\Lambda^2\mathfrak m\to\mathfrak{so}(8)=\mathfrak k\oplus\mathfrak m$ is $\mathfrak k$-equivariant, and $\Lambda^2V_7\cong\mathfrak{so}(7)$ is irreducible of dimension $21$, not isomorphic to $V_7$; hence its image lies in $\mathfrak k$. So $\mathfrak{so}(8)=\mathfrak k\oplus\mathfrak m$ is a $\mathbb Z_2$-grading, and

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{spin}}:=+1\ \text{on}\ \mathfrak{spin}(7),\quad -1\ \text{on}\ \mathfrak m$$

is an involutive automorphism of $\mathfrak{so}(8)$.

**It is not $\mathrm{Ad}_g$ for any $g\in O(8)$.** Suppose it were. By Steps 5 and 6 its fixed algebra would be $\mathfrak{so}(p)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(q)$ or $\mathfrak u(4)$; by dimension $21$ the only candidate is $p=1$, $q=7$, i.e. $\mathfrak{so}(7)$ embedded as $V_7\oplus\mathbb R$ on $\mathbb R^8$. Conjugation by $g\in O(8)$ carries the $\mathbb R^8$-module structure of a subalgebra to that of its conjugate (the map $v\mapsto gv$ is an isomorphism intertwining the two actions along $\mathrm{Ad}_g$), so it preserves reducibility of $\mathbb R^8$ as a module over the fixed algebra. The standard $\mathfrak{so}(7)$ acts reducibly on $\mathbb R^8$; $\mathfrak{spin}(7)$ acts irreducibly. Contradiction.

**Therefore the claim as written ("$n\ge3$, conjugate under $O(n)$"), is false, and $n=8$ is its only counterexample dimension.**

**But the statement survives at $n=8$ once conjugacy is taken in $\mathrm{Aut}$.** Let $G:=\mathrm{Out}(\mathfrak{so}(8))\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(D_4)=S_3$ by §3.2. $\mathrm{Inn}=\mathrm{Ad}(SO(8))$ and $PO(8)/\mathrm{Inn}$ is generated by the class $\bar\tau$ of a reflection, of order $2$; so $PO(8)$ is the full preimage of $\{1,\bar\tau\}$. Let $\sigma$ be any involution. Its image $\bar\sigma$ has order $1$ or $2$.

- If $\bar\sigma=1$ then $\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}\subset PO(8)$, and Steps 4–6 apply.
- If $\bar\sigma$ has order $2$ then $\bar\sigma$ is a transposition of $S_3$ lying in $G$. Every subgroup of $S_3$ containing a transposition is $\{1,\bar\tau\}$ or $S_3$. In the first case $\mathrm{Aut}=PO(8)$ and there is nothing to do. In the second, all three transpositions are $S_3$-conjugate, so there is $\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}$ with $\overline{\alpha\sigma\alpha^{-1}}=\bar\tau$, hence $\alpha\sigma\alpha^{-1}\in PO(8)$, and Steps 4–6 apply to it.

So **every involution of $\mathfrak{so}(8)$ is $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(8))$-conjugate to one of the five standard ones**, and the list of fixed algebras up to isomorphism is unchanged. This is the sense in which "Cartan's classification" is true at $n=8$, and it is exactly the sense the record's own convention row warns about.

The one on-file corroboration that $D_4$ is the exceptional diagram is [Wolf 1968](https://archive.ymsc.tsinghua.edu.cn/pacm_download/117/6051-11511_2006_Article_BF02394607.pdf), which twice treats it as the unique exception among the classical algebras, "If $\pi$ is not the triality automorphism of $D_4$, we may multiply it by an inner automorphism and assume that $\sigma$ is $-I$ on the Cartan subalgebra…" and "Finally, for $D_4$, triality now has determinant 1 because its cube has determinant 1." That is a lead, not a proof of anything above; the derivation stands on §3 and the $\mathfrak{spin}(7)$ construction.

## Step 9, the three conjugacy notions

Write $\mathcal A$ for conjugacy in $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(n))$, $\mathcal O$ for conjugacy by $\mathrm{Ad}(O(n))=PO(n)$, $\mathcal S$ for conjugacy by $\mathrm{Ad}(SO(n))=\mathrm{Inn}$. Then $\mathcal S\Rightarrow\mathcal O\Rightarrow\mathcal A$, and:

| pair | when they agree | when they differ, and the object |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathcal A$ vs $\mathcal O$ | every $n\ge3$ with $n\ne8$, by Proposition 1 | **only $n=8$.** $\sigma_{\mathrm{spin}}$ of §8 is $\mathcal A$-conjugate to the $\mathfrak{so}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(7)$ involution and lies in no $\mathcal O$-class at all. Separately, $\mathcal A$ merges classes $\mathcal O$ keeps apart, since $\mathfrak u(4)$ and $\mathfrak{so}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(6)$ are isomorphic |
| $\mathcal O$ vs $\mathcal S$ | all odd $n$; all $n\equiv2\pmod4$ | **exactly when $4\mid n$**, on the $\mathfrak u(n/2)$ class only |

**Proof of the second row.** The $\mathcal O$-class of $\mathrm{Ad}_g$ splits into two $\mathcal S$-classes precisely when the normaliser $N_g:=\{h\in O(n):hgh^{-1}=\pm g\}$ is contained in $SO(n)$.

- For $g_p=\mathrm{diag}(-1^p,1^q)$, $N_{g_p}\supseteq O(p)\times O(q)$, which contains elements of determinant $-1$ whenever $p+q=n\ge1$. So no split, at any $n$ and any $p$.
- For $g=J_0$, the centraliser is $U(m)$, and every complex-linear orthogonal map has real determinant $|\det_{\mathbb C}|^2=1$, so $U(m)\subset SO(2m)$. The anticommuting part is a single coset: taking $s=+1$ on the $e_i$ and $-1$ on the $f_i$ gives $sJ_0s^{-1}=-J_0$, so $\{h:hJ_0h^{-1}=-J_0\}=sU(m)$, on which the determinant is constantly $\det s=(-1)^m$. Hence $N_{J_0}\subseteq SO(2m)$ iff $m$ is even.

So for $n=4m'$ there are **two** $SO(n)$-conjugacy classes of involutions with fixed algebra $\mathfrak u(n/2)$ and one $O(n)$-class. The smallest case is $n=4$, where they are the involutions attached to the self-dual and anti-self-dual orthogonal complex structures $J_+$ and $J_-$, swapped by any orientation-reversing isometry, and by nothing in $SO(4)$. **At $n=12$ the same split occurs** ($m=6$ even), giving $9$ classes up to $SO(12)$ against $8$ up to $O(12)$.

*Note for the record's own warning row.* The convention file says the classification "differs from $SO(n)$-conjugacy at $n=8$". That is right about $n=8$ for the $\mathcal A$-versus-$\mathcal O$ gap, but **it is not the whole of the $\mathcal S$ story**: the $\mathcal O$-versus-$\mathcal S$ gap is at every $n$ divisible by $4$, including $n=12$, $16$, $20$, and it is invisible at $n=8$'s companion dimensions $n\equiv2\pmod4$. A consumer that needs $SO(n)$-conjugacy at $n=12$ is exposed to a gap that has nothing to do with triality.

## What a consumer may cite

The record's use is the enumeration of $\mathbb Z_2$-gradings of $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ at $n\ge12$. The following is what §§0–9 establish, with every hypothesis on the face of it.

> **Theorem (citable form).** Let $n\ge12$ and let $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ denote the **compact** real form, the real skew-symmetric $n\times n$ matrices. Let $\mathfrak{so}(n)=\mathfrak g_0\oplus\mathfrak g_1$ be a $\mathbb Z_2$-grading, equivalently let $\sigma$ be the involutive automorphism that is $+1$ on $\mathfrak g_0$ and $-1$ on $\mathfrak g_1$. Then:
>
> 1. $\sigma=\mathrm{Ad}_g$ for some $g\in O(n)$, unique up to sign, and $g^2=\pm I$.
> 2. $\sigma$ is conjugate, by $X\mapsto hXh^{-1}$ for some $h\in O(n)$, to **exactly one** of
>    - $\mathrm{Ad}_{g_p}$ with $g_p=\mathrm{diag}(-1^p,1^{n-p})$ and $0\le p\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$, for which $\mathfrak g_0=\mathfrak{so}(p)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(n-p)$ and $\mathfrak g_1\cong\mathbb R^p\otimes\mathbb R^{n-p}$, $\dim\mathfrak g_1=p(n-p)$;
>    - if and only if $n$ is even, $\mathrm{Ad}_{J}$ for an orthogonal complex structure $J$, for which $\mathfrak g_0=\mathfrak u(n/2)$ and $\mathfrak g_1\cong\Lambda^2_{\mathbb C}\mathbb C^{n/2}$ as a real module, $\dim\mathfrak g_1=\tfrac n2(\tfrac n2-1)$.
> 3. The number of classes is $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ for odd $n$ and $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+2$ for even $n$, the identity ($p=0$) included.
> 4. The isomorphism type of $\mathfrak g_0$ determines the class.
>
> **Hypotheses, all of them.** (i) $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is the compact real form, the statement is different for $\mathfrak{so}(p,q)$ with $pq>0$. (ii) $n\ge3$ and $n\ne8$; $n\ge12$ implies both. (iii) "Conjugate" means by $\mathrm{Ad}(O(n))$; at these $n$ this coincides with conjugacy in the full automorphism group $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(n))=PO(n)$. (iv) It is **strictly coarser than $SO(n)$-conjugacy when $4\mid n$**, where the $\mathfrak u(n/2)$ class is a single $O(n)$-class and two $SO(n)$-classes; at $n=12$ the $SO(12)$-count is $9$, not $8$.

**Two things a consumer may not do with this.**

- **Do not separate classes by $\dim\mathfrak g_0$.** By §7, $\dim(\mathfrak{so}(m-k)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(m+k))=m^2-m+k^2$ for $n=2m$, so it equals $\dim\mathfrak u(m)=m^2$ whenever $m$ is a perfect square. The first instance above $n=8$ is $n=18$, where $\mathfrak{so}(6)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(12)$ and $\mathfrak u(9)$ both have dimension $81$. Separate by isomorphism type (Proposition 2), or by the pair $(\dim\mathfrak g_0,\dim Z(\mathfrak g_0))$.
- **Do not extend the statement to $n=8$ with $O(8)$-conjugacy.** It is false there, by $\sigma_{\mathrm{spin}}$.

## What would falsify the statement, and what I did to look for it

**The falsifier is an involutive automorphism of $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ that is not $\mathrm{Ad}_g$ for any $g\in O(n)$, or one that is but whose fixed algebra escapes the list.** Two places such an object can hide, and what closes each:

1. *An automorphism moving the vector representation.* This is the whole content of Step 3, and the search there is exhaustive rather than heuristic: §3.2 bounds $\mathrm{Out}$ above by a finite, explicitly computed group (the diagram automorphisms) and §3.3 checks the vector node in every type $A_1$, $B_m$, $D_m$ that occurs. The single escape is $D_4$, and I built the escapee explicitly rather than asserting it: $\sigma_{\mathrm{spin}}$ of §8, from $\mathrm{Cl}_{0,6}\cong M_8(\mathbb R)$ and the branching $\Lambda^2\Delta\cong\mathfrak{spin}(7)\oplus V_7$, the latter derived here from a dimension count plus the exclusion of trivial summands, not quoted.
2. *A second fixed algebra inside a branch.* Closed by Steps 5 and 6, which compute $\mathfrak{so}(n)^{\mathrm{Ad}_g}$ from $g^2=\pm I$ with no case left over: $g^2=+I$ is diagonalisable over $\mathbb R$ with eigenvalues $\pm1$ and nothing else; $g^2=-I$ has a single $O(n)$-orbit, by the explicit induction on $g$-invariant planes.

I also probed the two ways a consumer could think the statement stronger than it is, and both probes found something: the fixed algebra can coincide across the two families ($n=8$ only, Proposition 2), and its **dimension** can coincide at infinitely many $n$ (whenever $n/2$ is square, first at $n=18$). Neither breaks the theorem; the second breaks a plausible way of using it.

**What I could not close, and am not claiming.** I did not establish that triality actually *merges* the $O(8)$-classes of $\mathfrak{so}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(6)$ and $\mathfrak u(4)$ into one $\mathrm{Aut}$-class. The dimension bookkeeping constrains it, the order-$3$ element permutes the four inner $SO(8)$-classes preserving $\dim\mathfrak g_0$, whose values are $16,12,16,16$, so it either acts trivially or $3$-cycles $\{\mathfrak{so}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(6),\ \mathfrak u(4)_+,\ \mathfrak u(4)_-\}$, and the spinor branching $\Delta_8^+|_{\mathfrak{so}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(6)}\cong(\Delta_2^+\otimes\Delta_6^+)\oplus(\Delta_2^-\otimes\Delta_6^-)$ would settle it as the $3$-cycle, but I have no on-file source for that branching and did not derive it. **It is not used anywhere above**; the $\mathcal A$-versus-$\mathcal O$ separation rests on $\sigma_{\mathrm{spin}}$ alone, which is derived in full.

## Verdict

**PROVEN, hypotheses corrected.**

The statement is proven for every $n\ge3$ with $n\ne8$, with conjugacy by $O(n)$, for the compact real form; and for every $n\ge3$ with conjugacy by $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}(n))$. **As literally written ($n\ge3$, conjugacy by $O(n)$), it is false, at $n=8$ and only there.** The record's four bare invocations are safe if and only if each is at $n\ne8$ and none of them consumes $SO(n)$-conjugacy at a dimension divisible by $4$; the $n\ge12$ consumer is safe on the first count and must be checked on the second, since $12$, $16$ and $20$ are all divisible by $4$.
