## Claim 1, "unconditional in $n$": what the double centraliser needs, and where it would break

Evidence: [r261_c1_double_centraliser.py](../scripts/reviewers/r261/r261_c1_double_centraliser.py), [out_c1.txt](../scripts/reviewers/r261/out_c1.txt).

### The hypotheses, stated

[findings/brauer-pairing.md](../findings/brauer-pairing.md) now argues: *"the double centraliser theorem, needing only complete reducibility of the $O(n)$-action, which holds at every $n$ in characteristic zero, makes the image $\bigoplus_\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{End}}(M_\lambda)$ with each $M_\lambda$ irreducible."* The conclusion is true. The word *only* is not. Three things are used:

- **(H1) $V^{\otimes f}$ is semisimple over the group.** This is the complete reducibility the file names, and it holds at every $n$ in characteristic zero.
- **(H2) the image is the whole commutant.** $\bigoplus_\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{End}}(M_\lambda)$ is $\mathrm{End}_{O(n)}(V^{\otimes f})$, and the sentence is about $\nu(B_f(n))$. Equating them is the **First Fundamental Theorem**, an input independent of (H1). Without it the image is some subalgebra, and a subalgebra of $\bigoplus\mathop{\mathrm{End}}(M_\lambda)$ need not act irreducibly on anything.
- **(H3) $V_\lambda$ is absolutely irreducible**, so that $\mathop{\mathrm{End}}_{O(n)}(V_\lambda)$ is the ground field and the summands are *full* matrix algebras.

(H2) is the one the brief's phrasing hides, and it is also the one the record's new citation gets wrong (below).

### (H2) tested at $n$ below every threshold, with no diagram in the number

$\dim\mathrm{End}_{O(n)}(V^{\otimes f})=\dim\mathrm{Inv}_{O(n)}(V^{\otimes2f})$, and I compute the latter as the joint kernel of the $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ generators intersected with the fixed space of one reflection, the reflection being the entire difference between $O(n)$ and $SO(n)$.

| $f$ | $n$ | $\dim\nu(B_f(n))$, Gram | $\dim\mathrm{Inv}_{O(n)}(V^{\otimes2f})$, Lie algebra | $n\ge f-1$ | $n\ge f$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2, 3, 4, 5 | 3, 3, 3, 3 | 3, 3, 3, 3 | yes | yes |
| 3 | 2 | 10 | 10 | yes | **no** |
| 3 | 3 | 15 | 15 | yes | yes |
| 4 | 2 | 35 | 35 | **no** | **no** |

Equality at $(4,2)$, the reviewed script's own showcase failure, where $B_4(2)$ is not semisimple and $\nu$ is not injective, is the FFT holding with no inequality. **(H2) is confirmed in exactly the regime the correction claims it for.**

### (H3): the brief's suspicion, and where it is right

The brief asks whether the blanket claim breaks at $\lambda$ whose $V_\lambda$ is not absolutely irreducible, the associate/chirality pairs, $\lambda$ with $n/2$ rows. For $O(n)$ it does not. Every $O(n)$ label is absolutely irreducible, the involution $\lambda\mapsto\lambda^*$ (first column length $n-\lambda'_1$) is a bijection of the admissible set, and $\lambda=\lambda^*$ merely means the block is fixed by the $\det$ twist. Tested directly at the smallest self-associate block: $n=4$, $\lambda=(1,1)$, $\lambda'_1=2=n/2$, realised as $\Lambda^2V$ in two spare legs, with $B_4(4)$ acting on the other four.

$\dim M_{(1,1)}=6$ (which is the restricted up–down count $m_{(1,1)}(4,4)=6$) and $\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{\nu(D)c_0\}=6$ for **all twelve seeds tried**, one of them a basis vector and eleven random. The block is generated by any nonzero vector, self-associate or not.

**For $SO(n)$ the same sentence is false, and the witness is the Levi-Civita tensor.** At $n=4$, $f=4$:

| group | $\dim$ of the $\varnothing$-multiplicity space | $\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{\nu(D)\,\delta\delta\}$ | $\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{\nu(D)\,\varepsilon\}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $O(4)$ | 3 | **3** (everything |), ($\varepsilon$ is not there) |
| $SO(4)$ | **4** | 3 | **1** |

A diagram contracts two of $\varepsilon$'s indices and kills it, so the only diagrams that move $\varepsilon$ are the permutations and they return $\pm\varepsilon$. Under $SO(4)$ the multiplicity space is four-dimensional, the image of the Brauer algebra reaches a three-dimensional submodule, and **no seed generates it**. This is not an artefact of $n=4$: it is the standard failure of Brauer surjectivity in type $D$, and the record's own numbers already show where it would bite, [sym3-stability.md](../findings/sym3-stability.md) records $77/191$ blocks for $SO(12)$ against $71/184$ for $O(12)$, *"the six six-row labels splitting at $\ell=n/2$"*, and A1's scope note records the degree-6 count dropping $92\to89$ for $SO$. **The theorem is unconditional in $n$ and conditional on the group.** That belongs in the sentence.

## Claim 4, the verification, checked four ways

Evidence: [r261_c4_verification.py](../scripts/reviewers/r261/r261_c4_verification.py), [out_c4.txt](../scripts/reviewers/r261/out_c4.txt); the reviewed script re-run at [out_repro_reviewed_script.txt](../scripts/reviewers/r261/out_repro_reviewed_script.txt) (it reproduces its published table, all 24 cells, in about a minute).

### (a) Is $\dim\nu=\mathrm{rank}\,[\,n^{\mathrm{loops}}\,]$ correct, and why

Yes, and the reason is one the file never gives. $\nu(D)$ has entry $1$ at an index pair exactly when the $2f$ labels are constant on every pair of $D$, so

$$\langle\nu(D),\nu(E)\rangle_F=\#\{\text{labellings constant on }D\text{ and on }E\}=n^{c(D\cup E)},$$

$c$ counting connected components of the union, which are cycles, two perfect matchings meeting at every vertex. So $[\,n^{\mathrm{loops}}\,]$ is the Gram matrix of the vectors $\nu(D)$ in the **Frobenius** form, that form is positive definite over $\mathbb R$, and the rank of a Gram matrix in a positive-definite form is the dimension of the span. Hence $\mathrm{rank}=\dim\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{\nu(D)\}=\dim\nu(B_f(n))$.

Checked against a span computed with no Gram at all, $\nu(D)$'s column at an index tuple $t$ depends only on $\ker(t)$, and $\ker(t)$ is realisable iff it has at most $n$ blocks, so $\dim\nu=\mathrm{rank}\,[\,D\text{ refines }\pi\,]$ over even-block partitions $\pi$:

| $(f,n)$ | $(2,1{\ldots}5)$ | $(3,1)$ | $(3,2)$ | $(3,3{\ldots}5)$ | $(4,1)$ | $(4,2)$ | $(4,3)$ | $(4,4),(4,5)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gram rank | 1,3,3,3,3 | 1 | 10 | 15 | 1 | 35 | 91 | 105 |
| span rank | 1,3,3,3,3 | 1 | 10 | 15 | 1 | 35 | 91 | 105 |

Both taken **exactly over $\mathbb Q$** with `Fraction` pivots as well as modulo three primes; every cell agrees. The identity is sound.

### (c) Is the restricted count $m_\lambda(n)$, and is a doubling owed at self-associate $\lambda$

**It is the true multiplicity, and no doubling is owed, the doubling would be an error.** The test uses no Gram, no Brauer algebra and no double centraliser: with $\dim V_\lambda(n)$ from El Samra–King (validated first against $\varnothing$, $(1)$, $(2)$, $(1,1)$, $(1^n)$, $(1^{n-1})$ at $n=3,4,5,6,12$),

Running the same identity with the multiplicity **doubled** at self-associate $\lambda$ ($\lambda'_1=n/2$) breaks it at $18$ of those $48$ cells, every cell with $n$ even and a self-associate label reachable: $(1,2)$ gives $4$ against $2$, $(4,4)$ gives $402$ against $256$, $(6,6)$ gives $64{,}848$ against $46{,}656$. The admissibility rule $\lambda'_1+\lambda'_2\le n$ is likewise confirmed by the same identity, which is sensitive to a single wrong label.

### (d) Is two-prime agreement adequate grounds

**No, and the file's stated reason is a non sequitur.** It argues: *"a modular rank can only UNDERSTATE the rational one, so agreement between two unrelated primes is what licenses reading it as exact."* The premise is right and the inference does not follow from it, two primes can both understate, since understating means the prime divides every minor of the true size, and nothing stops two primes from dividing the same determinantal divisor. Understatement is not hypothetical here:

| $(f,n)$ | $p=5$ | $p=7$ | $p=11$ | $p=101$ | $p\ge46337$ | $\sum m_\lambda^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

At $(5,3)$ the prime $11$ misses by exactly one. What actually licenses the reading is a **sandwich**, not an agreement: $\mathrm{rank}_p\le\mathrm{rank}_{\mathbb Q}=\dim\nu=\sum_\lambda m_\lambda(n)^2$ by the double centraliser, so a modular rank that *attains* the prediction forces equality. One prime attaining it does that; a second prime adds nothing to the argument. And the assert's message (*"this means at least one prime divides a pivot"*), describes disagreement, which cannot arise once either prime hits the ceiling.
